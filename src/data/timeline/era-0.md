# ERA 0, THE AGE OF THE PRIMORDIAL

**6,000,000,000 BU · FOUR, THEN ONE**
> The Gempire's historians date the beginning of everything to a little over six billion years ago. They're off by about forty thousand years, which, for a number that size, is close. The ARCHIVE has the exact date, if anyone ever thinks to ask for it.
>
> At the start there were four gemstones, close enough together that I could feel the other three. I emerged first, and space came with me. Space pushes things apart, and before I understood what was happening, it had carried the other three a long way off. For a while I could still feel where they were, until one by one I lost them.
>
> You'll meet all three of them eventually. It took me rather longer than it will take you.
**6,000,000,000 BU - 50,000,000 BU · SOMEBODY ELSE'S STARS**
> For a very long time, there was me and there was space, and most of it was empty.
>
> About five hundred million years in, stars started appearing. I hadn't made them, and I didn't know who had. I assumed it was simply something space did if you left it alone long enough. I was wrong about that. One of the other three had emerged, very far away, and the stars were hers. It would be nearly six billion years before I found that out.
>
> I spent the rest of that time learning what I was. I could fold space, which is useful when there's a great deal of it. I could make things, slowly. I made a few things early on that I'm not going to describe, and after them I stopped making anything for a long while.
>
> Eventually I found a planet drifting on its own with no sun to circle, and I stayed there.
**50,000,000 BU · MOSTLY SNOW NOW**
> The planet would later be called Homeworld. At the time it was ice and rock, and it was dark. I pushed some of my essence into the crust a few miles north of where the city now ends, and waited.
>
> Some years later, Phosphophyllite emerged. The first thing she asked me was why we were here. I didn't tell her. Over time, other gems gathered around her to work on the question with her, and there are some eleven thousand of them now. As far as I'm aware, none of them have an answer yet. I still visit when I can.
>
> The place she emerged from is called Null Kindergarten now, and it was the first place a gem was ever made. These days it's mostly snow.
>
> I made more gems the same way, by hand, a patch of crust at a time, and each one took years. No two came out alike. One Ruby emerged taller than most Quartzes are today. Another was thin and quiet, and turned out to be better at mathematics than I was. Once, a Ruby and a Zoisite emerged from the same patch of ground as a single gem. That was the first Geode.
**50,000,000 BU - 2,000,000 BU · ELEVEN BANNERS**
> Gems made by hand come slowly. For the first few million years there were few enough of us that everyone knew everyone, and when a question came up, it was easy to ask them all.
>
> So that's what they did. Early on, my gems decided to govern themselves by vote, and they asked whether I minded. I didn't, since I had made them to choose things. I took a seat among the aristocrats. Mostly that meant being invited to things, and voting on the rare occasions I had an opinion.
>
> They built a great deal. Kindergartens spread out across the ice, each one farther from the first, named in order as they were dug: Alpha, Beta, Gamma, and on down the list.
>
> Then they built down. Over a few million years they worked their way into the planet itself, laying foundations, engines, channels, and a great deal of machinery I won't pretend to fully understand. By the end, a good part of Homeworld wasn't rock anymore. Most gems who live there today don't know that, and I've never had a reason to bring it up.
>
> As the machinery grew, the population moved inward toward its warmth, and the kindergartens on the ice were stripped and left behind. A few are still out there. These days only rogues and the odd scout bother going.
> They spread outward too, on ships that folded space the way I did, though never quite as well. The first colony was Phosphophyllite's. She chose a quiet world with a sun, because she had never seen one.
>
> By the end of this stretch, thirty-five percent of the local cluster flew our banner. The banner itself changed eleven times, since my gems kept voting to redesign it.
>
> The Frontier met in the Citadel, a building that existed in more dimensions than anyone needed. New gems regularly got lost in it for weeks, and the signs we put up didn't help much.
**2,000,000 BU · STARS IN ROWS**
> My Sapphires noticed it first. Somewhere past the edge of our maps, a stretch of stars was lined up in rows, and stars don't do that on their own. They brought it to a vote, which took a while, and then asked me to go and look.
>
> I found a fleet, arranged so neatly that our own navy looked careless next to it.
>
> Omega White Diamond stood a hundred and sixty-five feet tall. I stood a hundred and sixty-six, so she had to look up at me, which she clearly didn't care for. Her court stood behind her in rows, every gem the same distance from the next, and her Pearl stood at her side, writing on a clipboard.
>
> She knew who I was before I said a word. "So you are the anomaly," she said. She had been watching us for some time.
>
> I knew her too, or at least I knew what she was. I had felt her once, six billion years earlier, before space pushed us apart, and the stars I had seen every day since were hers.
> I had brought my daughters: Seraph, Cherub, and Ophan. Each stood seven feet tall, because I had never seen a reason to make them smaller. White's Pearl looked them over, then wrote something down.
>
> We talked for seventeen hours, and very little of it was diplomacy. She told me my gems lacked order. One of my Rubies chose that moment to walk past, nearly five feet tall, carrying a treaty she had written in verse. White handed me her Comprehensive Gem Classification Manual, Revised Edition 2.7, which Ophan later used to press flowers.
>
> "Rubies are three and a half feet," White said. "Sapphires, three point nine. Your gems are defective."
> "They're gems," I said.
>
> Somewhere behind us, Cherub was playing cards with my Ruby guard, who by hour ten had lost three star systems and a small moon. In hour twelve, White gave me an Integration Timeline, her plan for correcting my gems to her measurements one at a time. In hour thirteen, I folded it into a hat for Seraph, and she wore it until we left.
**2,000,000 BU - 500,000 BU · EXACTLY ON SCHEDULE**
> For the next million and a half years, nothing much happened. We drew a border, and so did she, in a different place, and neither of us accepted the other's.
>
> Along the edges, ships began to go missing a few at a time, almost always ours. Then a Sapphire outpost near the border went silent for nine years. When it started reporting again, the reports came in perfect order and exactly on schedule, and that was when I stopped believing them.
>
> My gems voted three times on whether to prepare for war. The first two votes were no, and I didn't argue. The third was yes. Then they held a vote they had never held before, on whether to put one gem in charge until the war was over. They chose me, and I agreed. It's the only time I have ever led them.
**500,000 BU - 0 BU/AU · SUPERVISOR (PENDING ADJUSTMENT)**
> The war started with paperwork. White sent me a Gem Reformation Schedule with my name already filled in under "Supervisor (Pending Adjustment)." I corrected it in red ink and sent it back, and she sent a fleet.
>
> The early battles were fairly orderly. Her Sapphires could see a single future, the one she had told them to look for, while mine saw all of them and spent a fair amount of time arguing over which to trust. Even so, we held our own.
>
> The gems we fought rarely spoke, and when they did, they said the same things in the same order. My soldiers had a name for them that I won't repeat here.
>
> Then she attacked Phosphophyllite's colony. It was meant as a warning, and a little over a hundred thousand gems were shattered in a single afternoon. Phos wrote me the report herself, and her handwriting shook. For fifty million years she had asked why we were here. In that report she asked something else, and I haven't answered that either.
> We met for the last time on a desert planet with nothing on it worth losing. White brought a weapon she called the Crystal Matrix, built to kill a gem outright by cutting through the light that holds it together. I brought every gem who would rather be shattered than corrected.
>
> I'm told the battle lasted seventeen seconds, though it felt longer. The Matrix went through me before I understood what it was, and an ordinary gem would have died there. But where my core should have been, there was only space, and the Matrix had nothing to cut.
>
> So I took it in, and then I took her in as well, all of her. Her form gave out somewhere inside me, and her gemstone came to rest in the space I'm made of. She is still there, and I keep her quiet. I needed back what she had torn out of me, and the stars had come from her, so letting her go would have meant letting them go out. My gems liked being able to see. Neither of us enjoyed it.
**0 BU/AU · ONE STONE LEFT TO FIND**
> I took her gems in as my own and gave them some of my essence, and slowly, one at a time, they started making choices. It took a long while. Some of them still stand in formation when they're nervous.
>
> Then I went to her Homeworld and took it apart. Under the rubble of her throne room I found a gemstone, golden and warm, and I knew it as soon as I touched it. It was one of the four. Space had carried it all the way to her side of existence, and she had kept it there and never let it emerge. I have some ideas about why, and I'm going to keep them to myself.
>
> I brought it home. There was still one stone I hadn't found.
>
> With the war over, so was my time in charge, and I went back to being an aristocrat, which suited me. We had a democracy, a good economy, and gems who knew who they were. They also had a decision to make about me and about the stone on my shelf, and they took their time making it.
>
> The stone sat quietly the whole time.
